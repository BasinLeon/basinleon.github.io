A Second Brain Still Needs Judgment

Why indexing years of notes with AI only matters when a human still owns the taste, the filter, and the final call.

Leon Basin · October 6, 2026

I’ve been gathering years of drafts, notes, and projects into Google Drive and exploring them with Gemini.

I started because I wanted to find my own work again.

There were writing drafts beside technical projects, campaign material beside old notes, and folders whose names suggested more order than their contents could confirm. Some work was ready to use. Some was unfinished. Some needed to remain private.

Calling it a second brain gave me a useful shorthand. Building it has made me more careful about what that phrase promises.

A system can hold a document without preserving the circumstances that made it useful.

Was this a proposal or a result? Was the number measured or estimated? Did I finish the project, or did I only describe what I intended to build?

Those distinctions matter when an old file becomes the basis for a new decision.

What belongs beside the file

In sales and operating work, a note can become an action quickly. A remembered conversation becomes an account plan. A draft becomes a handoff. An assumption becomes a forecast.

If the context disappears, the next person may inherit confidence the original work never earned.

That is the leadership question inside this archive experiment: what should travel with the information?

For the next small collection I organize, I want to preserve four things:

  • The original source, so I can inspect it again.

  • Its status: draft, proposal, completed work, or unverified claim.

  • Its date, so an old conclusion does not quietly become current advice.

  • Its boundary: public, private, or suitable for sharing only after review.

These are the rules I want to test. They are not a claim that every file in my archive already meets them.

Asking a better question

I want to ask Gemini to connect related material, then check those connections against the originals.

“What do these files suggest?” is a useful starting question. I also need to ask: “What is missing? Which source supports this? Where are two versions disagreeing?”

A fluent answer can feel finished before the underlying work is settled. Keeping the source nearby gives me a way to examine that feeling.

The same practice belongs in a team. When someone presents a recommendation, I want to understand the evidence and the uncertainty well enough to take responsibility for the next step.

One small test

My next experiment is deliberately bounded: one collection of notes, one question, and one written piece.

I’ll look at whether I can find the source again, whether the connection helps, and whether I still agree with the conclusion after checking it.

The writing matters here too. An archive can surface a fragment, but I still have to decide whether it belongs in an essay, a play, an operating document, or nowhere public.

I build systems for work. I write about the life those systems cannot contain. Working on this archive has put those two parts of my practice next to each other.

I want a second brain that helps me return to the work with better questions.

What context would someone need before acting on the most important document in your team's shared drive?
