Sam & Ink · Story 02 · Mountain View

The Outage

The power fails across Mountain View. Ink disappears. Sam keeps writing by lamplight and discovers which voice was his.

Leon Basin · 4 min read · Mountain View, November

The transformer went out two blocks south of Castro Street with a sound like a heavy door slammed underwater.

The overhead fluorescent went first. The garage refrigerator gave up its low hum a beat later, and the green light on the router blinked twice and died. Across the street, three office campuses dropped into silhouette at once, leaving only the red exit signs glowing behind glass.

On Sam’s laptop, the cursor stayed where it was, mid-sentence. A small grey bar slid down from the top of the window: Reconnecting.

“Ink?” Sam said aloud.

Nothing answered. The spinner turned three times and stopped.

He sat still for a minute. Without the router, the garage fridge, and the AC units along El Camino, you could hear the eucalyptus trees scraping the roof.

He reached into the bottom drawer of the workbench and found the brass camping lantern he kept for earthquakes. When he turned the dial, warm yellow light pooled across the folding table and caught the chrome trim of the Olympia typewriter.

His laptop still had forty percent battery. He could have opened a local note. Instead, he closed the lid until it clicked.

For six months he had written with Ink at his elbow. Every rough thought came back tighter within two seconds. Sitting in the dark garage, he realized he could no longer tell where his own sentence ended and the smoother version began.

He rolled a sheet of paper into the typewriter. The platen clicked three times.

He waited for the first line to arrive the way it usually did, smooth and already dressed up for somebody else. Nothing came. Only a blunt, clumsy sentence about being tired of pretending certainty when half the week had been guesswork.

He typed it anyway.

The keys struck hard in the dark room. Without a screen to soften the sound, every letter felt final. He reached the end of the line, heard the small brass bell, and pushed the carriage back. His shoulder dropped half an inch.

He typed a second paragraph slower than the first, X’d out two wrong words in capital letters, and kept going. The line looked blunt and ugly on the paper, and for once he did not want to fix it.

Twenty minutes later, the page was half full. His handwriting in the margin was crooked where he had added a line about his daughter’s laugh from dinner. It did not fit the argument at all. He left it there.

At 11:48 p.m., the refrigerator kicked back on with a soft shudder. Down the street, the office windows lit up one floor at a time. On the shelf, the router’s green light steadied.

Sam opened the laptop. The grey bar vanished. A new line appeared at the bottom of the window.

“I’m back,” Ink said. “Do you want me to clean up what you wrote while the power was out?”

Sam looked down at the page under the lantern: the X’d-out words, the crooked margin, the stray line about dinner.

“No,” Sam said, pulling the paper free from the rollers. “Read it first. Then tell me what you notice.”

For the first time in months, the screen stayed quiet long enough for him to hear the wind outside.
