The Weak Signal Is the Real One

A weak signal is not a bad signal. Most of the time it is an early one.

Leon Basin · November 10, 2026

Every scoring system I have used is good at one job. It finds more of what already worked.

Feed it last quarter's wins and it will find accounts that look like last quarter's wins. Feed it strong intent and it will rank strong intent at the top. That part works. I rely on it.

The problem is what it leaves out. The deals that changed a territory for me rarely started strong. They started as a line in a document nobody else had read closely, or a comment in a public meeting that did not sound like a buying signal at all.

A weak signal is not a bad signal. Most of the time it is an early one.

Strong signals arrive crowded

When a signal is obvious, everyone sees it at the same time.

A city posts a formal request for proposals. A company announces a new leader in a press release. A budget is approved in public. By then the inbox is already full. Five vendors have the same alert. The conversation starts as a contest, not as a discussion about the problem.

I have lived that race. You move fast, you send a clean note, and you still sound like everyone else because you all read the same headline on the same morning.

Strong signals matter. You should work them. You just should not mistake them for the whole job. If your system only catches what is already loud, your pipeline will always be late and always be crowded.

The work that holds up over time comes earlier, when there is still room to be useful before a formal process starts.

What a weak signal looks like in the field

In public sector work, a weak signal rarely looks like demand. It looks like friction.

A staff report names the same intersection for the third time. A council member asks a short question about response times and then moves on. A public works director says the current process takes too long, in a meeting about something else. None of that scores well. There is no budget line yet. There is no project name. A model trained on closed deals would pass it over.

A person who knows the territory reads it differently. The repetition matters. The question matters because of who asked it and what committee they sit on. The complaint matters because it came from the person who would own the fix.

I keep a separate list for this kind of thing. It is not pipeline. I do not forecast it. I write down what I heard, where I heard it, the date, and what would make it real. A follow up item on a future agenda. A study getting funded. A new hire in the right seat. That note takes two minutes. Without it, the early signal is gone in a week, buried under louder work.

The same pattern shows up outside cities. The best book I read last year had a handful of reviews. The operator who taught me the most about pipeline had a small audience and no interest in growing it. Early things look small in the data. That is why the data misses them.

Hold the weak signal without lying about it

The risk on the other side is just as real. If you treat every weak signal as pipeline, your forecast becomes fiction. I have done that version too. A long list, a lot of hope, and nothing a manager can trust.

So I hold weak signals in a different way than strong ones. Three rules keep me honest.

First, name it as a watch item, not an opportunity. The label changes how you work it. A watch item gets a check date, not a sequence. You do not send four emails because a staff report mentioned a street once.

Second, write the trigger that would promote it. Be specific. Not "follow up later," but the actual event: the item returns to committee, the study is approved, the grant window opens. If the trigger does not happen, the item stays on watch or it comes off the list. It does not drift into pipeline on hope.

Third, do the human read yourself. Weak signals need context that a score cannot carry. Read the full report, not the summary. Watch the ten minutes of the meeting where the topic came up. That read is slow. It is also the whole advantage. Anyone can buy the alert. Few people do the reading before the alert exists.

What changes when you keep that space open

A territory run this way feels different. You still work the strong signals, and you work them fast. But you also arrive early to a small number of real problems, with something specific to say because you saw the problem form.

Those conversations sound different from the first minute. You are not pitching a category. You are talking about a crossing, a corridor, a process the person in front of you already named in public. Trust builds faster when the other person can tell you did the work before you needed something from them.

That is the gap I try to protect in every system I build. Automate the loud signals so they take less of the day. Score them, route them, act on them quickly. Then spend the time you saved on the quiet part of the list, where judgment still beats the model.

The algorithm will keep showing you more of what you already are. A good operator goes looking for what the territory is becoming.

Keep a watch list. Read it every week. Let the weak signals stay weak until they earn their way up. When one does, you will be the person who saw it first, because you were paying attention before it was easy to see.
