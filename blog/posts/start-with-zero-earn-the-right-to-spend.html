Start With Zero. Earn the Right to Spend.

The tool was never the missing piece. The motion underneath it was.

Leon Basin · October 27, 2026

Every small team I have worked on has had the same conversation. Pipeline feels thin, so someone suggests a new tool. A data platform. A sequencing tool. An intent product. The demo looks clean. The dashboard in the demo always looks clean.

I have bought that hope before. The tool arrives, the logins get passed around, and two months later the team is having the same conversation about thin pipeline, now with one more invoice and one more dashboard nobody trusts.

The tool was never the missing piece. The motion underneath it was.

Buy the tool after the work works

A tool amplifies what is already there. If your scoring is honest, a tool can score more accounts. If your scoring is a guess, a tool will guess at scale and call the result a list.

That is the order I hold to now. First prove the motion by hand. Then spend money to remove the manual parts that slow it down.

When I started working a new territory in Colorado and Northern California, the first version of the system was unglamorous. A sheet for accounts. Public sources for signals. My own inbox for outreach. It was slow in the places where slowness taught me something. I had to read the council agenda myself. I had to decide whether a job post meant a real initiative or just a backfill. I had to write each first message without a sequence doing the writing for me.

That manual pass is where the judgment gets built. Skip it and you automate a motion you do not understand yet.

Constraint makes the work better

When every email costs you time, you choose the account with care. You do not email a city because it appeared on a list. You email it because you saw something specific: an agenda item, a new hire, a safety problem a resident raised in a public meeting. The message can name that thing because you actually looked.

Free sources reward that kind of looking. Council agendas are public. Job posts are public. Local news is public. Conference conversations cost nothing except showing up and listening well. None of it arrives scored and sorted. That is the point. Sorting it is the skill.

An expensive stack reverses the pressure. Once a team has paid for ten thousand records, someone feels obliged to use ten thousand records. Volume becomes the justification for the purchase. Outreach gets broader and thinner at the same time, and the replies get worse in a way the dashboard struggles to explain.

I would rather send twenty messages I can defend, one by one, than two hundred I have to apologize for. On a small team, attention is the budget. Spend it where the signal is real.

Know what spending is actually for

Spending has a place. It comes after the manual version produces conversations over several weeks, running the same play.

At that point, name the bottleneck in plain words. If the bottleneck is judgment, a tool will not help you. More data will not tell you which account matters. If the bottleneck is mechanics, spending starts to make sense. Collecting the same public records by hand every morning, cleaning the same fields, copying the same notes from one place to another: that is work a tool should carry.

The test I use is simple. Can I describe the play, the input, and the reply it produced, without mentioning a product? If yes, the motion is real and a tool can make it faster. If the description falls apart without the product name in it, I do not have a motion yet. I have a subscription looking for a purpose.

There is a second test, and it is the one teams skip. Will the tool still earn its cost in a slow month? Selling to cities slows down as the holidays approach. Budgets close, councils meet less often, and people take the time they have earned. A stack bought for peak volume sits there billing through the quiet weeks. A lean stack, built from a sheet and public sources and a disciplined inbox, costs almost nothing to keep alive while the market catches its breath.

What the real stack is

The real stack is the part no vendor sells. It is knowing which signals matter in your territory and which ones are noise. It is the discipline to work the list you built instead of shopping for a new one. It is writing a first message a person might actually answer.

Software can support each of those. It cannot supply them.

So start with zero. Build the ugliest version that works: a sheet you trust, a short list of public signals you check on a schedule, and outreach you write yourself, account by account. Run it until the pattern is clear enough to describe to someone else. Then buy the one tool that removes the slowest mechanical step, and only that tool.

Earn the right to spend, one proven step at a time. The engine you build that way will still be standing when the demo dashboards are gone.
