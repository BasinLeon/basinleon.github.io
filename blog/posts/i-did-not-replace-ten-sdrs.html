I Did Not Replace Ten SDRs. I Removed the Wrong Work.

The system did not replace ten people. It removed work that should never have needed ten people in the first place.

Leon Basin · November 17, 2026

The headline version is easy to tell wrong.

I built a system that monitors market signals, enriches them, scores them, and routes the good ones to a rep with context attached. It processes more in a day than a team of ten SDRs could work in the same time. People hear that and hear replacement.

That is not the part that matters. The system did not replace ten people. It removed work that should never have needed ten people in the first place. That difference decides whether a GTM system helps a team or hollows it out.

Most SDR work was never selling

Look at what fills an SDR's week in a traditional motion. Checking the same accounts again to see if anything changed. Copying company facts from one tool into another. Reading a funding headline three days after it posted. Writing a first note from a blank page, with no context except a name, a title, and a quota clock running.

None of that is selling. Selling starts when a person understands a problem well enough to talk about it plainly. Everything before that is monitoring, sorting, and data entry wearing a sales title.

We measured that work as activity because activity is easy to count. Calls made. Emails sent. Accounts touched. The count went up and the pipeline did not care, because most of the activity started too late and carried too little context to change a conversation.

The real problem had a name. I call it human latency: the gap between a signal appearing in the market and a person acting on it. A company announces a round or hires a new leader. The signal is public on day one. The human motion finds it on day three, researches it on day four, and sends on day five. By then four competitors have already sent the same note off the same headline.

You cannot fix latency by asking people to work faster at tasks machines do better. You fix it by taking those tasks off their plate entirely.

What the machine actually does

The system I built, the Signal Refinery, runs in four steps, and none of them involve talking to a customer.

First, it watches. It monitors more than fifteen sources for the movements that matter: funding, leadership changes, hiring patterns, incidents, regulatory shifts. It does not find a signal late.

Second, it enriches. A raw headline is not enough to act on. The system adds the facts a rep would otherwise spend an hour gathering: size, funding status, tech stack, the right contacts, and the history we already have with the account.

Third, it scores. Not every signal deserves a human. A verified intent signal scores high. Noise scores near zero and goes nowhere. In my build the scoring held at 0.8 precision, so reps could trust most of what reached them. Trust is what makes a routing system survive past week two.

Fourth, it routes. A qualified signal lands with the right rep in about 400 milliseconds, with context attached and a recommended next step. The rep's first job is no longer research. It is judgment: is this real, and what do I say that is actually useful?

That is the whole design. The machine ends where the human work begins.

What people do with the time back

When monitoring and sorting leave the job, the job changes shape.

Onboarding got shorter in my experience, from about ninety days to about five, because a new rep no longer had to learn a research ritual first. The system carried the ritual. The rep learned the territory and the customer instead.

Conversations got better too. A rep who starts with context is not asking questions the public record already answered. They can open on the problem the signal pointed to, in the customer's own terms, while it is still current.

And the pipeline result followed the quality, not the volume. The systems I built supported 160 percent year over year U.S. pipeline growth. I do not credit the machine alone for that number. I credit what happened when skilled people stopped spending their best hours on work a script could do.

That is the test I now apply to any AI tool in a sales motion. Does it give time back for judgment, or does it just produce more activity to manage? A tool that generates a thousand more emails has made the latency problem worse. It has industrialized the wrong work.

How to start without building the whole thing

You do not need my architecture. Start smaller.

List the ten to fifteen signals that actually predict a conversation in your market. Not the signals your tool happens to sell you. The ones you have seen precede real deals. Name the source for each one.

Then separate your team's week into two columns. Machine work: monitoring, enrichment, first pass scoring, routing. Human work: reading the account closely, choosing the angle, having the conversation, following through. Most teams find research sitting in the human column out of habit, not necessity.

Automate one signal end to end before you automate ten badly. One signal, watched well and routed with context, will teach you more than a dashboard full of half wired alerts. Measure it on precision and on time from signal to first human touch.

Only then scale it. And protect the boundary. The moment the system starts writing and sending on its own, you have not removed the wrong work. You have removed the person from the one part of the job where a person is the point.

The machine handles the signals. Humans handle the relationships. Keep those two jobs separate, and the system makes the team stronger. Blur them, and you get faster noise.

I would rather have a smaller team with time to think than a large one paid to be late. That is what removing the wrong work actually buys.
