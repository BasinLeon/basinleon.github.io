One Win Is Not a System

A win you cannot repeat is not pipeline. It is luck with good notes.

Leon Basin · November 24, 2026

Every operator has a story about the play that worked once.

Mine usually starts in a public place where buyers talk about a problem before they have a budget for it. A council agenda posts. A staff report names a problem in plain language. A city manager changes seats. Someone asks a question in a meeting that tells you the work has already started, even if no vendor has been called yet.

You follow that signal. You do the research by hand. You write a note that fits the moment because you were close enough to see it clearly. A conversation starts.

That feels like proof. It is only an experiment that passed its first test.

The mistake is what happens next. Most teams celebrate the win, tell the story in the next pipeline review, and then go back to volume. The play that worked stays in one person's head. The next rep starts from zero. The next territory starts from zero. A month later nobody can say why it worked, only that it did, once.

A win you cannot repeat is not pipeline. It is luck with good notes.

The experiment is the easy part

Running a small test is not hard. Pick one hypothesis and keep it narrow enough to prove or kill.

In public sector work the hypothesis is rarely about a product feature. It sounds more like this: when a city puts a safety problem on a public agenda twice in one quarter, a real conversation is possible now, not next year. Or: when a new leader inherits an old problem, they want a clear baseline before they want a pitch.

So you watch a short list of accounts. You read the agendas yourself. You note the exact words staff used, because those words are the language your first message should use. You reach out to a small number of people, with the observation first and the ask second.

Then you stop and count honestly. How many replies came from people who own the problem, not from people being polite? How many asked a second question? How many said the timing was wrong but the problem was real? That last group matters more than most teams admit. A no on timing with a yes on the problem is a future account with a date attached.

If you cannot answer those questions, you did not run an experiment. You sent some messages and kept the feeling.

Measure what changed a conversation

Most activity reports measure effort. Calls made. Emails sent. Accounts touched. None of that tells you whether the play deserves to live.

Measure the play on three things.

First, response quality. Did the reply come from the person who feels the problem, and did it use their words back to you? That is a different class of reply from a referral to a generic inbox.

Second, time. How many days passed between the public signal and your first useful touch? In govtech the window is wider than in commercial sales, but it still closes. A budget cycle turns. A consent calendar fills. Another vendor gets the first briefing and sets the terms everyone else has to answer.

Third, repeatability. Could a second person run the same steps on a different city and get a conversation of the same quality? If the answer depends on you being clever that morning, you have a talent, not a system. Talent does not transfer at a handoff. A system does.

Write those numbers down while the work is fresh. Memory edits them later, always in your favor.

Systemize the part that worked, and only that part

Systemizing does not mean automating everything. It means writing down the steps that produced the result, in order, so the result stops depending on memory.

For a signal play, the written version is short. Name the signal and where it appears in public. Name who owns the problem when that signal fires. Write the first message pattern, with the observation in the account's own language and one clear next step. Set the follow-up rule: what new fact earns a second touch, and what silence means you park the account and watch instead of pushing.

Then set the review point. After ten accounts, read the replies as a set. Keep the step that earned the second questions. Cut the step that only produced opens. Change one thing at a time, or you will not know what fixed it.

This is the unglamorous work. It is also the whole difference between a territory that resets every quarter and a territory that compounds. In my current work across Colorado and California, the accounts that move are rarely the ones we contacted most. They are the ones where a public trigger, a named owner, and a timely first conversation were written down and run the same way the second and third time.

Start with your last three wins

You do not need a new tool to start this. You need your last three real conversations and an honest hour.

Write down, for each one, the signal that started it, the exact first step you took, and the reply that told you it was real. Compare the three. The shared steps are your system trying to exist. The one-off steps are decoration.

Take the shared steps and write them as a play someone else could run tomorrow morning. Run it on ten accounts. Measure response quality, time from signal to touch, and whether the second run matched the first.

If it holds, you have the start of an engine. If it falls apart, you learned something cheaper than a quarter of random activity would have taught you.

One win feels good. A system is what lets the next win arrive on schedule, run by whoever holds the territory, long after the first story has been told.
